Why does India's dry heat give way to downpours that transform farms, rivers, and daily life?

The monsoon is a seasonal reversal of winds, organizing a season of changing weather.

Summer land heats faster than the ocean, helping create lower pressure over the continent.

Moist air flows towards this lower pressure, carrying ocean water inland as invisible vapour.

The tropical rain belt shifts northward, helping organize the summer circulation over India.

Moisture arrives through the Arabian Sea and weather systems over the Bay of Bengal.

As moist air rises, it cools; water vapour condenses into clouds that can produce rain.

The Western Ghats force approaching winds upward, enhancing rain on slopes facing the moist flow.

Leeward areas receive less rain, creating a rain shadow and making rainfall vary across regions.

The southwest monsoon typically reaches Kerala around early June, during the June to September rain season.

Rain alternates between active spells and breaks, partly following shifts in the monsoon trough.

El Niño often reduces rainfall, while La Niña can increase it; these tendencies offer no guarantee.

For farmers and reservoirs, monsoon rainfall is vital, linking seasonal weather with food and water.

From October to December, the northeast monsoon brings important rain to southeastern India, including Tamil Nadu.

Northeasterly winds can acquire moisture crossing the Bay of Bengal, bringing rain to that coast.

Understanding the monsoon means connecting seasonal heat, shifting winds, ocean moisture, rising air, and terrain.
